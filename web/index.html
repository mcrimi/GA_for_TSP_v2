Breeding a shorter route

In 2020 I gave a route-finding algorithm a trick from plant breeding: start with a crowd of candidates and narrow it to a few. Here I test that trick properly. It does little. What plant breeders actually do works very well.

The idea

A salesman has to visit every city once and come home. Which order makes the trip shortest? With 194 cities there are more possible orders than atoms in the universe, so nobody checks them all. A genetic algorithm breeds an answer instead:

  1. Start with a few hundred random tours: the population.
  2. Keep the shortest ones. This is selection.
  3. Pair the survivors at random and cross each pair: build a child tour out of pieces of both parents.
  4. Now and then mutate a child: change it a little at random.
  5. Survivors and children are the next generation. Repeat.

I was working with plant breeders at the time, and a wheat breeding programme has a famous shape. It starts with a couple of million plants and, year after year, keeps fewer of them, until fifty lines go to trial and one becomes a variety. Breeders call it the selection funnel. So I made my population do the same: large at first, shrinking every generation.

My write-up at the time was candid: the results were “not far off” what a fixed population gave, “but I thought it would be fun to try”. I never measured it. This page does.

What I found

Two things make the test fair. The maps are real countries, and their shortest possible tours are known, so every result can be stated as “this much longer than the best there is”. And every method gets exactly the same amount of work, the 269,800 tour evaluations the 2020 program spent. Each figure below is the average of 40 runs.

1. The funnel is not what matters

On Qatar the 2020 program ends 189% above the shortest tour: nearly three times too long. Against a fixed population of the same average size the funnel makes no measurable difference (+2 points, an interval that includes zero). Simply choosing a better size does far more: with a fixed 50 and more generations the same program reaches 141%.

Djibouti is kinder to the idea. There the funnel beats its same-average rival (−5 points), and matches the best fixed size, which on this small map is the largest I tried. So the funnel is not nothing. It behaves like a population of about its average size, sometimes a little better, and it is usually better than growing instead of shrinking. But the size that works best swings from a thousand to a few dozen between two maps, and getting that right is worth far more than the shape.

2. What matters is how tours are crossed, changed and refined

Keep the budget and change one thing at a time. A mutation that reverses a stretch of the tour, instead of swapping two cities, takes Qatar from 148% to 28%. A crossover that builds children from the parents’ roads, rather than from the positions of cities, reaches 37% on its own. And letting each child tidy itself up before it is judged, by uncrossing any two roads that cross, brings the answer to within 0.1% of the shortest possible.

That last step is not a triumph of local tidying alone. Tidying random tours for the same budget, with no breeding at all, stops at 1.6%. Crossing good tours and then refining the children is what gets the rest.

3. Why the 2020 crossover failed: every cross costs, and the population inbreeds

Breeders have a word for what goes wrong when two good but different lines are crossed and the offspring are worse than either: the cross broke up combinations that worked. I measured it. Take a population of decent tours, cross random pairs, and compare children with parents. With the 2020 crossover the children are 50% longer than their parents; with the two alternatives, 8% and 9%. Early in a run the difference is starker still: one cross in 200 beats its better parent under the 2020 crossover, against one in 4 for the crossover that keeps roads. The 2020 crossover hands down 79% of the parents’ roads and fills the gaps with random ones, and one random road across Qatar costs as much as several good ones.

The second effect follows from the first. If nearly every real cross is a disaster, the only children that survive selection are those of near-identical parents. The population fills with copies of its best member: by the end of a 2020 run the survivors share 96% of their roads with the best tour. Breeders call this inbreeding, and it ends progress for the same reason in a field as in a program: there is no variation left to select from.

4. What the breeder’s funnel really says

Looking again at the breeding diagram I had copied, I had read the wrong thing off it. The funnel is not a population that shrinks while it keeps interbreeding. It starts from one cross. Everything after that is generations of sorting out and fixing what the cross produced, with the cheap tests done on the many and the expensive ones on the few.

In an algorithm, “cross once, then spend your effort refining the offspring” has a name: a memetic algorithm, a genetic algorithm with local search inside. It is the step that took Qatar from 24% to 0.1%. The breeders were right. My translation was wrong.

With refinement in place I gave the shrinking population one more chance, and here the answer depends on how much work there is to spend. With the full budget on Qatar the funnel is the best setting I tried: 0.07% on average, and the true shortest tour in 22 of 40 runs. With a quarter of the budget it loses to a small fixed population, and on Uruguay, where the same budget has to cover 734 cities, it is four times worse (4.0% against 1.0%). A wide start is a luxury: worth having when work is plentiful, a waste when it is not.

I also tried the funnel’s other half, cheap screening for the many and thorough refinement for the few. It was never the best setting (0.4% on Qatar).

5. Was it original?

No, on both counts, though I did not know it then.

Bringing breeding to genetic algorithms was done thoroughly in 1993, when Mühlenbein and Schlierkamp-Voosen published the Breeder Genetic Algorithm, with truncation selection and the breeder’s equation for predicting the response to selection. Populations that change size go back at least to Arabas, Michalewicz and Mulawka in 1994. And the exact rule I used, shrinking the population in a straight line over the run, is the “linear population size reduction” of Tanabe and Fukunaga’s L-SHADE, which won a major optimisation competition in 2014.

The funnel’s other half, cheap evaluation for many candidates and careful evaluation for few, is how modern hyperparameter search works under the name successive halving. That is the place where the breeding funnel really pays, because there, as in a field trial, measuring a candidate properly is expensive. Measuring a tour is not, which is why the idea had so little to grip on here.

What I would keep from the exercise is the breeder’s habit of measuring. How much does a cross cost? How inbred is the population? Those two numbers explained the 2020 result better than anything in the algorithm’s settings.

How it was built

The 2020 version was a single Python script that scored open paths rather than round trips, so its results could not be compared with known answers. This one is a tested package. The inner loops are compiled with Numba: the Qatar run that took eight and a half minutes in 2020 now takes a fifth of a second, which is what makes thousands of runs, and so honest comparisons, affordable.

The run at the top of this page is the same algorithm, ported to JavaScript. Distances are whole numbers and both versions take their randomness from one small generator built from integer arithmetic, so the same seed gives the same run in both. A test replays runs exported from Python, generation by generation, and requires the browser version to match every number.
